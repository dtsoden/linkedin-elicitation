# LinkedIn format

What's known for certain, then what people widely believe but LinkedIn hasn't confirmed. Draft to the first set. Treat the second set as worth testing, not as rules.

## Known

A post can hold up to 3,000 characters. A comment can hold up to 1,250.

The feed shows only the first few lines before the "see more" cut. On a phone that's often around 200 characters, depending on line breaks. The checker prints roughly what will show above the cut. The specific claim, range, or question should start there, or at least be clearly on its way, because a reader who doesn't open the post can't reply to what's in the middle.

Text styled with Unicode "bold" or "italic" characters (𝗹𝗶𝗸𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗶𝘀) is read aloud letter by letter or skipped by screen readers, and it doesn't show up in search. The checker blocks it.

## Believed, not confirmed

These come from people who post a lot. LinkedIn hasn't published them as rules, so log results and decide based on your own numbers.

- Posts with an outside link in the body reach fewer people. A common workaround is to put the link in the first comment.
- A handful of hashtags does as well as or better than a long string. The checker warns above three.
- Replies in the first hour or so after posting help a post travel. Whether or not that's true, fast replies keep a thread going while people are still reading it, so it's worth doing anyway.

## Shape

- Short paragraphs of one to three sentences. Mobile readers see a wall of text as homework.
- Plain text, no headers. Bullets only when the content is a list.
- Avoid ending on a question unless the post is an expert call-out. A strong middle draws replies without one.
- 600 to 1,300 characters suits most elicitation posts. Longer is fine when the middle needs it. Padding pushes the claim further from the cut.
