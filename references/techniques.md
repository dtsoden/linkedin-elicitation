# Techniques

Seven ways to give readers something to respond to. The key in brackets is the name `log.py` uses. The example posts show shape only. Their numbers are placeholders, and a real post takes its numbers from `facts.md`.

Every post follows the same hourglass shape. Open with something broad that the reader recognizes from their own work. Narrow down to the one specific claim, range, or question that draws replies. Close with something short and light that doesn't summarize the post or ask for anything. The middle does the work. Keep the opening and closing short so the middle isn't lost.

## Range [range]

State a range from your own experience and let people place themselves in it. Practitioners can't resist giving their own number, so the comments become a small survey.

Needs: a real range from the facts file, ideally from your own clients or projects.
Risk: a range so wide it says nothing ("1 to 100 hours"), or one you made up to look like an insider.

> Client onboarding at the agencies I've worked with has taken anywhere from 6 to 14 hours of staff time. The ones near 6 had a single intake form and one kickoff call. Everyone above 10 was chasing logins for a week.

## Debatable claim [debatable]

A position you actually hold and know some peers would dispute. This replaces the "deliberate false statement" from elicitation playbooks, which isn't allowed here. The test: if a smart person pushes back, would you argue the point honestly?

Needs: an entry under "Positions I hold" in the facts file.
Risk: dressing up a safe opinion as edgy ("unpopular opinion: be nice to clients"), or a hot take you'd drop the moment someone challenged it. Limited to two in 30 days.

> I think most B2B case studies would get more replies with the results section cut. Buyers skim to the number, don't believe it, and leave. The before-and-after workflow is the part they forward to their boss.

## Skeptical take [skeptic]

Say plainly that you doubt something others claim works. The people it does work for will show up and explain how, which is often the most useful comment thread you'll get.

Needs: an honest doubt, ideally one where you'd like to be proven wrong.
Risk: sneering. Doubt the claim, not the people who make it. "I don't buy it" works. "Anyone who says this is lying" doesn't.

> I've yet to see cold DMs on LinkedIn close a deal above five figures for a services firm. I've seen them book calls. I haven't seen those calls turn into signed work at any real size.

## Insider framing [insider]

Write as if the reader already knows the basics, and go straight to the part only experienced people run into. Experienced readers feel addressed and add their own fix.

Needs: a specific problem from your work that beginners haven't hit yet.
Risk: jargon that shuts out the rest of the audience. One unexplained term is fine. Five makes a wall.

> Everyone running Meta ads has hit the learning-phase reset after a "small" budget change. What I haven't settled is whether duplicating the ad set beats waiting it out once spend is above a few hundred a day.

## Learner post [learner]

Describe a real problem you haven't solved. People love teaching, and a specific problem gets specific answers.

Needs: an entry under "Things I'm still figuring out" in the facts file. It must be true.
Risk: the most dangerous technique here. An expert playing at being lost gets caught, because readers check your title and history. Limited to one in 60 days.

> Two years into posting here and I still can't predict which posts travel. My two best performers this year were both written in ten minutes. The one I spent a week on got eleven reactions.

## Give first [give-first]

Share a real number or result from your own business. Readers match it, because sharing feels safe once someone else goes first.

Needs: an entry under "Numbers I can share" that the user has cleared for public posting.
Risk: a number that's flattering but not representative. Share the ordinary one.

> Our close rate on inbound leads this year is 22 percent, and on referrals it's 61 percent. I knew referrals were better. I didn't expect almost three times better.

## Expert call-out [expert-callout]

Address a specific group and ask what they've seen. It works because it picks out the people who know and gives them a reason to show it.

Needs: a group narrow enough that its members recognize themselves, and a question you actually want answered.
Risk: a group so broad it's flattery ("leaders, what's your secret?"). Name the group by what they've done, not by title.

> Bookkeepers who've handled S-corp elections for first-year LLCs: what's the mistake you see owners make most often before they come to you?

## Matching technique to facts

| Facts file has | Good fits |
|---|---|
| Ranges or benchmarks from your own work | range, give-first |
| Positions you'd defend | debatable, skeptic |
| A specialist audience | insider, expert-callout |
| A real open problem | learner, expert-callout |
